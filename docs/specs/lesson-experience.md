# Lesson experience: how a lesson teaches (owner, Oct 24, 2026)

The owner learned with lesson 1 on his iPhone and wrote:

> "The first lesson also doesn't distinguish between a phrase and a word. Guten is a word, Guten Tag is a combination of words. It also finishes the lesson with a bunch of mixed ways of testing understanding. The very first lesson throws you into sentence building, typing in an answer yourself. And it feels unstructured. Duolingo for example always includes the words to build the sentence with. We cannot try to include a one for all. The lesson also exposed you to words not even covered initially on the lesson. It overall feels undone and lackluster."

These rules apply to **every lesson from now on**, and lessons 1 to 9 are reworked to meet them (story agent, one lesson per run, starting with lesson 1). They sit inside the existing lesson format (scene → notice → words → check → build → conversation → done); what changes is what each step asks of the learner.

## 1. Words and phrases are different things

- A **word** is one word: *gut*, *Tag*, *Morgen*. A **phrase** is a fixed combination: *Guten Tag*, *Wie geht's?*, *Einen Moment, bitte*.
- A phrase card says it is a phrase and shows its parts: *Guten Tag* = *guten* (good) + *Tag* (day), with a one-line note when the grammar is worth a word ("*guten*, not *gut*: the ending changes before *Tag*; you'll see why later").
- The word list in a lesson counts phrases and words separately in the card header ("Phrase 2 of 4", "Word 3 of 8"), and a lesson's 10 to 14 items may mix both, but each item is labelled.
- Teach the parts of a phrase as words when they're useful on their own (*Tag*, *Morgen*), not before.

## 2. Never test what wasn't taught

- Every German word the learner has to **produce or choose** in a lesson (check options, build tiles, gap answers, the learner's own lines in the conversation) has been introduced in this lesson or an earlier one.
- Partner lines in the conversation may contain at most one or two new words, always glossed, and never ones the learner then has to use.
- Wrong options (distractors) come from words already met, preferably the same kind (greetings against greetings).
- quality adds a test that enforces this for every built-in lesson.

## 3. One clear ladder of difficulty

Within a lesson, the steps climb in a fixed order, and the learner always knows what kind of task comes next:

1. **Meet:** word and phrase cards (listen, see, one pronunciation aid).
2. **Recognise:** hear or see the German, pick the meaning. Then the reverse: see the English, pick the German.
3. **Assemble with help:** build a sentence from a **word bank** of tiles that contains every word needed, plus at most two distractors from known words.
4. **Produce:** fill a gap or type, only once the word has been seen in steps 1 to 3, and always with the word bank available as "Show words".

Across the course the ladder moves up gradually:
- **Lessons 1 to 3:** no typing at all. Build and gap items use tiles only; the conversation uses tiles or choosing a line.
- **Lessons 4 to 10:** gap items may ask for one typed word the learner has already used in this lesson; tiles stay the default.
- **From lesson 11:** typing whole short sentences, with the word bank one tap away.
- Never "a bunch of mixed ways" at the end: the build step uses one or two exercise types, in a set order, and the conversation is the finale, not a new test format.

## 4. Feedback in the same voice as the app

Right and wrong answers use the app's own type and colours (no separate style for the feedback bar), say each thing once, and tell the learner something useful: the English, the rule, or what was wrong ("*Guten*, not *Gute*: before *Tag* it ends in *-en*").

## 5. Done means done

A lesson is finished when every step works on a phone with the keyboard open, nothing overlaps (especially the orb), all audio plays, and the owner could use it without a single "huh?". The headmaster checks lesson 1 again on a phone-size screen before saying it's fixed.
