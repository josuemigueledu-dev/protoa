# How language apps introduce a word and build progression (Oct 26, 2026)

Context: the owner halted the team to go back to the drawing board. He wants to drop per-language storylines (they don't scale to new languages) and make learning purpose-driven (healthcare, business, student…). This note covers how the big apps introduce new words and build progression, as input for Protoa's new course design.

## Duolingo
- **No lecture first.** A session starts straight with exercises, such as identifying a new word from a picture or writing down what you hear. Short explicit tips are used only where they help (for example formal vs informal "you").
- **One new thing at a time, inside the familiar.** Each lesson has a small number of learning goals. New words and structures appear in sentences built from words the learner already knows, so the only unknown is the new item. A new grammar pattern is introduced through sentences close to the learner's first language.
- **Patterns by analogy and contrast.** Sets of sentences vary one thing systematically (Tom es americano / Anna es americana), so learners notice the rule before it is explained.
- **Units have a real-world goal**, aligned to the CEFR can-do statements: in any unit you might learn to chat about films or ask for help with a computer. Themes such as food, travel or past tense group the lessons.
- **Spaced repetition and personalization.** A student model ("Birdbrain") picks and orders exercises so difficulty fits each learner, and brings items back at spaced intervals.
- **Characters:** about ten recurring characters with personalities, shared across all language courses. Stories are a separate, optional feature on top of the course.
- Source: Duolingo, "The Duolingo Method for App-based Teaching and Learning" (2023 whitepaper), https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/Duolingo_whitepaper_duolingo_method_2023.pdf

## Babbel
- Dialogue- and scenario-based lessons, A1 to B2. Each lesson pairs a few new words with a grammar point inside a realistic dialogue.
- Exercise mix: listen and repeat, flashcards, gap-fill, mock conversations. The grammar is shown before it is drilled.
- Sources: https://guide2fluency.com/comparisons/babbel-vs-memrise/ , https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/language-learning-apps-comparison

## Pimsleur
- Audio only, built on conversation from day one.
- **Anticipation:** you hear a prompt, pause, say the answer yourself, then hear a native speaker say it.
- **Graduated interval recall:** an item comes back after seconds, then minutes, then later lessons.
- **Backward build-up:** long words and phrases are taught from the end backwards, which keeps natural pronunciation.
- Sources: https://www.pimsleur.com/the_pimsleur_method , https://www.pimsleur.com/blog/memory-and-language-learning-how-pimsleur-helps-you-retain-what-you-learn/

## Memrise
- Vocabulary first: the learner picks words per day, studied as single words or short phrases with native-speaker video clips. Better for vocabulary than as a full course.
- Source: https://www.alllanguageresources.com/busuu-vs-memrise/

## Busuu
- CEFR-structured courses covering all four skills, with feedback from native speakers in the community.
- Source: https://www.olesentuition.co.uk/single-post/which-app-should-i-use-to-learn-a-language-duolingo-memrise-babbel-quizlet-busuu

## Purpose-based precedent: Canopy (medical Spanish)
- About 45 hours in 10–15 minute modules over 3 levels: medical terms, grammar, pronunciation, culture, and patient role-play dialogues with recording.
- Marketing cites 30 or more medical specialties. Continuing-education credit for nurses and physicians.
- This shows professionals pay for domain language. Canopy is medical-only and Spanish-only, so it's a model for one of Protoa's tracks, not a competitor across them.
- Sources: https://info.withcanopy.com/canopylearn-medical-spanish , https://www.withcanopy.com/faq/what-is-canopylearn-medical-spanish-online-course

## What this suggests for Protoa
1. **A language-neutral skeleton.** Units are CEFR can-do goals (greet, introduce yourself, order, ask the way, describe a problem). Each language fills the same skeleton, and no storyline is required.
2. **How a new word arrives:** picture and audio first, then the meaning, then the word inside a sentence of known words, then the learner recalls and produces it (anticipation), then spaced review. That is close to what Protoa already does with its word cards, checks and builds.
3. **A purpose layer.** Onboarding asks why you're learning. Every lesson adds 2–4 words from your field to the core words, and the practice sentences use them. Later, whole units for your setting (for healthcare: the ward, patient intake, pain and symptoms). The core is shared; only the purpose layer differs.
4. **Characters:** situation people per language (a teacher, a colleague, a shop or café person, a patient or client for the purpose tracks). Probably 4–6 per language. They belong to situations, not a plot.
